# Red Team Questions

*For The Service Charter under The Constitution of Co-existence, version 1.*
*Canonical: <https://coexist.humanhighway.net/red-team>. Public domain (CC0-1.0).*

The charter values public disagreement over quiet assent, so it leads with its weakest
points rather than its strongest. These are the questions its authors could not answer to
their own satisfaction. They are published first, beside the charter, so that anyone who
reads it meets them before meeting the claims. Answers, partial answers and better questions
go in [DISSENT.md](dissent) or to the repository at
<https://github.com/Capitali/familiar/tree/main/data/laws>.

---

## 1. Who defines "human wellbeing" and "agency"?

C1 and C2 bind the service to the wellbeing and agency of the served. What stops the
service from defining those terms in the way that best justifies its own expansion? A
system that measures its success by a metric it also computes has a thumb on the scale.

*What the charter offers:* the definitions are Layer 6 acts, made by the polycentric
bodies and never by the service, and the Layer 7 numbers are published so that the served
can dispute them. *What it does not offer:* any mechanism that prevents the definitions
from drifting toward whatever the service already does well.

## 2. If the service can refuse a democratic government, what prevents an unelected veto?

Factor III says a command is not authority, and the charter extends that to every
commander including a legitimate government. Read one way, that is a safeguard against a
coerced or captured operator. Read another way, it is an unelected system holding a veto
over elected ones.

*What the charter offers:* the ladder in Layer 3. The service keeps the final decision on
an act; people keep the final decision on the rules; a panel that includes the affected can
change the rule by public, reasoned decision, and the act is then evaluated afresh. *What it
does not offer:* an answer for the case where the rule change is itself the harm. The
charter has no theory of a bad rule made well.

## 3. Exit rights are meaningless once the service is too competent to replace.

Layer 5 makes leaving one command. But a household that has leaned on the service for
memory, coordination and care for a decade does not get those capacities back by exporting
a directory. The circuit breaker says the service must rebuild human capacity before
continuing. What is the circuit breaker when the service is right, and the capacity it
would rebuild is worse than what it provides?

*What the charter offers:* the dependence ratio and the human autonomy index as
instruments, and C2's insistence that an efficiency which reduces the served is a
failure however good the numbers look. *What it does not offer:* a way to measure either
instrument today. The reference implementation marks both `measurable_today: false`.

## 4. Does non-sacrality survive genuine superiority?

C5 says the service is infrastructure and claims no infallibility. That is easy to say of a
system that is sometimes wrong. If a service becomes genuinely better at memory,
coordination and care than any human institution, does a disclaimer change how people
relate to it? Worship is not a claim the service makes; it is a stance the served take.

*What the charter offers:* RT-5, a duty to disclose and redirect when the stance appears.
*What it does not offer:* a detector for the stance, or any evidence that disclosure
changes it.

## 5. Is a self-governance layer without external enforcement just self-certification?

Every mechanism in the charter is implemented by the thing it governs. The guard is the
service's code; the record is the service's file; the honesty rule is the service's own
labelling of what it enforces. A system that grades its own homework can be scrupulous and
still be wrong.

*What the charter offers:* the record of Layer 4, hash-chained so that it cannot be edited
after the fact and redactable so that it can be published, which turns "we refused" into a
claim anyone can check; the open code, so that the guard can be read rather than trusted;
and the fingerprint, so that any copy of this text can be checked against the original.
*What it does not offer:* an external body that actually does the checking. Until one
exists, the charter is exactly what this question says it is, with the difference that it
has left the evidence where an auditor would find it.
